Similarity metrics. 
Proposition 1. Q-gram cyclic similarity metric 
	To compare presentations p1 <r1, r2> and p’1 <r’1, r’2>, we will be considering respective pair of relators separately. Let s1 = |r1| + |r’1| (total length of the first relators) and similarly s2 = |r2| + |r’2|. Then similarity(p1, p2) = s1/(s1 + s2) * qgramdistance(r1, r’1) + s2/(s1 + s2) * qgramdistance(r2, r’2) 
	Qgramdistance (a, b) is calculated as follows:
	For each of an and b separately, q-gram multisets are constructed (call them Ma and Mb respectively). WLOG, consider relator a. We consider each 5 <= q <= 11 and consider all substrings, including cyclic substrings, of length q and insert them into Ma. 
	Then we calculate the multiset similarity on between Ma and Mb using Jaccard similarity. 

	Justification: insert-rotate moves have a block-changing nature, i.e. the changes made are not discrete by generators, unlike conjugation moves. Cyclic q-grams account for rotations. Jaccard similarity produces score in [0, 1] which means similarity is independent of string length. i.e. longer strings will not have higher similarity than shorter simply because they have more q-grams. 
	Considerations: 
	-	longer matching substrings should have more weight in similarity, because there is a smaller chance this structure was created “by chance”. Should we give weight to substrings? 
	-	AC2 moves allow for inverses. Should we consider an inverse of any substring equivalent to that substring in the multiset?

Proposition 2. Edit-distance with ‘weighted tokenisation’
	To compare presentations p1 <r1, r2> and p’1 <r’1, r’2>, we will be considering respective pair of relators separately. Let s1 = |r1| + |r’1| (total length of the first relators) and similarly s2 = |r2| + |r’2|. Then similarity(p1, p2) = s1/(s1 + s2) * tokeneditdistance(r1, r’1) + s2/(s1 + s2) * tokeneditdistance(r2, r’2) 
	tokeneditdistance(a, b) is calculated as follows: 
	Our aim is to divide an and b into substrings (i.e. tokenise them), so that the weighted edit distance between these ordered sets of substrings would be highest. Define dp[I][j] as the highest similarity when considering a[0…I] and b[0…j]. Let L be the largest L, such that a[I - L + 1…I] = b[j - L + 1…j]. dp[I][j] = max(max(k^2 + dp[I - k][j - k]), over 1 <= k <= L, dp[I - 1][j], dp[I][j - 1]), i.e. we try every 1 <= k <= L as a length for the token. Notice that k adds k^2 to the similarity score, i.e. the relationship between substring (token) weight and influence on similarity is quadratic. 
	Now, dp[|a| - 1][|b| - 1] is a relative measure of similarity between the two strings, however it is not normalised in the sense that higher |a| and |b| will naturally yield higher similarity, making it hard to compare two pairs of relators. To solve that, the final similarity score is dp[|a| - 1][|b| - 1] / (|a| + |b|) ^ 2

	Justification. Unlike classical AC moves, insert-rotate moves 1. Give higher weight to longer matching substrings and 2. Consider the relative order of common substrings. 
	Considerations. Doesn’t account for cyclicality.

Right, LCS (Longest Common Substring) is another similarity metric. 
LCS(p1, p2) for two presentations p1 and p2 is equal to the shortest total presentation length of product of r1 * r1'^{-1}; r2 and r2'^{-1} or r1 * r2'^{-1}; r2 and r1'^{-1}, given that all relators have been rotated. This is based on observation that when p1=p2, the product will have length 0 (as all terms will cancel), therefore we might want to try minimise the product length. 
Implementation (from https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/dsa/suffix-tree-application-5-longest-common-substring-2/) is based on an algorithm for finding the Longest Common Substring for each pair of relators. This is implemented with suffix trees in O(total presentation length).
